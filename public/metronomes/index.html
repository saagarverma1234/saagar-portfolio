How it works: why metronomes fall into sync

Put a group of metronomes on a board that can roll, start them at random, and within a minute or two they all tick together. This game runs the same equations as the Chaos to Order videos: nothing is scripted.

Each metronome is a self-driven pendulum

A metronome is a pendulum with an escapement: a small spring mechanism that gives the pendulum a tiny kick on every swing. If the swing gets too small, the kicks add energy; if it gets too big, friction wins. So every metronome settles into its own steady swing. Like real ones, each metronome in the game runs a tiny fraction of a percent faster or slower than its neighbours, so on their own they would drift apart forever.

The rolling board connects them

The board sits on rollers, so it can slide left and right. When a pendulum swings to the right, it pushes the board slightly to the left, just as stepping forward in a small boat pushes the boat backward. That is conservation of momentum. The board's motion in turn shakes every metronome on it. This is called coupling, and the board is the only link between them.

Here is the key effect. When most metronomes swing together, the board rocks in rhythm with them. A metronome that is slightly ahead of the crowd gets its base moved in a way that slows it a little; one that is behind gets sped up a little. Small nudges, repeated a hundred times a minute, pull everyone toward the common beat.

Phase locking and the Kuramoto picture

Physicists describe each metronome by its phase: where it is in its swing, as an angle on a circle (0° at one end, 180° at the other, back to 360°). Yoshiki Kuramoto showed in the 1970s that a crowd of oscillators with slightly different natural speeds will suddenly lock to a shared rhythm once the coupling between them is strong enough compared with how different they are. Fireflies flashing together and audiences clapping in unison follow the same rule. Metronomes on a board are the classic tabletop example, worked out by James Pantaleone in the American Journal of Physics in 2002, the paper this game's engine is based on.

What the numbers in the game mean

  • IN STEP % is the Kuramoto order parameter. Picture every metronome as an arrow pointing to its phase on a circle and average all the arrows. If they point every which way, the average is close to zero (0%). If they all point the same way, the average is a full-length arrow (100%). The game shows it averaged over the last half second so it doesn't flicker.
  • Synced in X s appears when the counter reaches 100% and stays there for half a second. The timer counts from the start of the round or your last action.
  • Speed (ticks a minute) is the tempo. A tick is half a swing, so 208 ticks a minute is about 1.7 full swings per second.
  • BEAT NOW, shown while the motor runs, is the tempo the crowd is actually keeping, measured from the average swing.

What each button does to the physics

  • Nudge gives the board one short shove. It disturbs everyone at once, which can break a stuck pattern.
  • Shake starts a motor that rocks the board at 1.1 times the metronomes' tempo (229 against 208). A strong steady drive can drag oscillators onto its own beat, a process called entrainment. Watch BEAT NOW to see whose beat wins.
  • Flip reverses a metronome's swing as it passes the centre, jumping it half a cycle. Tap a single metronome to flip just that one.
  • Add big one sets down a metronome 18 times heavier. It pushes the board 18 times harder than any other, so the crowd tends to fall in behind it.
  • Add weights puts 48 kg on the board. A heavier board barely moves, so the coupling becomes very weak and the metronomes stay out of step for a long time. Take the weights off and syncing resumes.

Why some ties never break

In the Break the 3-way tie challenge, three equal teams are a third of a swing apart. Their pushes on the board cancel exactly, like three people pulling a ring at 120° to each other, so the board stays still and nothing pulls the teams together. Remove that symmetry by flipping a few metronomes from one team, lifting some off or adding the big one, and the board starts to move and sync takes over. The 50 vs 50 tie (two teams half a swing apart) also cancels at first, but it is unstable: the tiniest imbalance grows, and left alone it breaks in about 15 seconds.

Is it exactly real?

The equations are the published model, solved with the 4th-order Runge-Kutta method in small time steps, using realistic masses and lengths. Only the drawing is exaggerated: the board's sway is shown at twice its real size so you can see it. You can try the same experiment at home with a few metronomes on a light board resting on two drink cans.
